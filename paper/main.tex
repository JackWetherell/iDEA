\title{\iDEA: an open-source Python framework for exact many-electron quantum mechanics and density functional theory in one dimension}

\author[york]{Jack Wetherell\corref{cor1}}
\ead{jack.wetherell@gmail.com}
\author[york]{Matt J. P. Hodgson}
\author[york]{Leopold Talirz}
\author[york]{Rex W. Godby}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author.}
\address[york]{Department of Physics and York Centre for Quantum Technologies, University of York, York YO10 5DD, United Kingdom}

\begin{abstract}
We present \iDEA{} (interacting Dynamic Electrons Approach), a free and open-source Python framework for the exact solution of the static and time-dependent many-electron Schr\"odinger equation for finite one-dimensional systems of a few electrons, together with approximate methods (non-interacting, Hartree, Hartree--Fock, a local density approximation and hybrid functionals) that share a common interface. Exact Kohn--Sham, and more generally exact fictitious-system, potentials are obtained by reverse engineering the exact ground-state or time-dependent density. \iDEA{} is designed for reproducibility, interactivity and simplicity. It has been used to provide exact benchmarks for density functional and many-body perturbation theory, to generate training data for machine learning, and to teach many-body quantum physics. We describe the methods and their implementation, validate the exact solver against analytic results, and illustrate the code with three examples whose figures are regenerated by a script distributed with the code.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Program summary}
\noindent\emph{Program title:} \iDEA{} (interacting Dynamic Electrons Approach), version 1.1.0\\
\emph{CPC Library link to program files:} (to be added by Technical Editor)\\
\emph{Developer's repository link:} \url{https://github.com/JackWetherell/iDEA}\\
\emph{Licensing provisions:} GPLv3 (or later)\\
\emph{Programming language:} Python ($\geq 3.8$; continuous integration with 3.10, also run with 3.13)\\
\emph{Supplementary material:} none\\
\emph{External routines/libraries:} NumPy~\cite{NumPy}, SciPy~\cite{SciPy}, Matplotlib, tqdm, JupyterLab; optionally CuPy~\cite{CuPy} for GPU acceleration.\\
\emph{Nature of problem:} Exact ground, excited and time-dependent many-electron wavefunctions of finite one-dimensional systems with arbitrary external potentials, interactions and spin configurations are needed as reference data against which approximate methods, such as Kohn--Sham density functional theory (DFT), time-dependent DFT and Hartree--Fock theory, can be assessed. The exact Kohn--Sham potential corresponding to a given interacting density is also required.\\
\emph{Solution method:} The many-electron Hamiltonian is represented on a uniform real-space grid using high-order finite differences, and the lowest eigenstates are found by sparse Lanczos diagonalisation followed by explicit antisymmetrisation of the space--spin wavefunction. Time propagation applies the matrix exponential of the Hamiltonian at each step. Approximate methods share a self-consistent single-particle solver. Exact Kohn--Sham potentials are found by iteratively inverting the ground-state or time-dependent density.\\
\emph{Additional comments including restrictions and unusual features:} One spatial dimension, uniform grid with hard-wall boundaries. The cost and memory of the exact solver grow as $N_x^N$ (and the antisymmetrised wavefunction as $(2N_x)^N$), restricting it in practice to two electrons on a few hundred grid points or three electrons on a few tens of points. The LDA is that of the spin-polarised electron gas. GPU acceleration applies to the exact solver only. An experimental Kohn--Sham strictly-correlated-electrons module is included. Installable via \texttt{pip install iDEA-latest}; documentation at \url{https://idea-interacting-dynamic-electrons-approach.readthedocs.io} and a tutorial notebook are provided with the source code.

\section{Introduction}

Approximate electronic-structure methods are routinely assessed against more accurate ones, but in three dimensions exact many-body solutions exist for only the smallest systems. Finite one-dimensional (1D) model systems offer an alternative: with a softened Coulomb interaction~\cite{Javanainen1988} they show many qualitative features of real atoms, molecules and materials~\cite{Wagner2012,Baker2015,Helbig2011}, yet the many-electron Schr\"odinger equation can be solved exactly for a few electrons. Exact solutions give access to the quantities that approximate theories try to model, most notably the exact Kohn--Sham (KS) potential of density functional theory (DFT)~\cite{HK1964,KS1965} and its time-dependent counterpart~\cite{RG1984,Maitra2016,Ullrich2012}. These are unknown in general and must be obtained by inverting the exact density~\cite{vanLeeuwen1994,WangParr1993,WuYang2003,Jensen2018,Kanungo2019,Shi2022,Nielsen2013,Ruggenthaler2015}.

\iDEA{} grew out of this programme in the group of R.~W.~Godby at the University of York. Exact time-dependent solutions and reverse-engineered KS potentials computed with \iDEA{} revealed static and dynamic steps in the exact exchange--correlation (xc) potential~\cite{Ramsden2012,Hodgson2013,Hodgson2016,Hodgson2017}, complementing related studies by others~\cite{Lein2005,Elliott2012}. The code has since been used to construct local density approximations (LDAs) from finite systems~\cite{Entwistle2016,Entwistle2018}; to assess hybrid functionals~\cite{Elmaslmane2018}, the GW approximation~\cite{Wetherell2018}, the nearsightedness of many-body perturbation theory relative to KS DFT~\cite{Wetherell2019} and generalized KS theory~\cite{Hodgson2020PRA}; to compute exact potentials for the fundamental gap~\cite{Hodgson2021PRA}; and to generate exact one-body density matrices for machine learning~\cite{Wetherell2020FD}, a use of exact 1D data pioneered elsewhere~\cite{Snyder2012,Li2016}. It is also used in teaching.

The current version, \iDEA{}~1.1.0, is a rewrite of the original research code as a pip-installable Python package with a small, uniform interface designed for reproducibility, interactivity (for example in Jupyter notebooks) and simplicity. Octopus offers exact many-body solutions of model systems within a large real-space TDDFT code~\cite{Octopus2020,Helbig2011}, density-matrix renormalisation group calculations give 1D benchmarks for larger systems~\cite{Wagner2012,Baker2015}, other packages treat lattice or second-quantised models~\cite{QuSpin2017,OpenFermion2020} or three-dimensional systems approximately~\cite{PySCF2020}, and n2v inverts densities supplied by other codes~\cite{Shi2022}. \iDEA{} combines exact real-space solutions, the standard approximate methods and density inversion, ground state and time dependent, in one lightweight package.

\section{Methods and implementation}

\paragraph{Systems}
A \texttt{System} is defined by a uniform grid $x_i$ with spacing $\delta x$, an external potential $\vext(x)$, an interaction matrix $v_\mathrm{int}(x,x')$ and a string of electron spins, e.g.\ \texttt{"uud"}. All examples here use the softened interaction $v_\mathrm{int}(x,x')=1/(|x-x'|+1)$ of earlier \iDEA{} work, e.g.\ Ref.~\cite{Hodgson2013}, in Hartree atomic units; a variant of the soft-Coulomb form $1/\sqrt{(x-x')^2+1}$~\cite{Javanainen1988}; both are provided. The kinetic energy is represented by central finite differences of 3 to 13 points (default 13). The truncated stencil implies hard walls at the grid edges, so the box must be large enough for the density to vanish there.

\paragraph{Exact solution}
For $N$ electrons the spatial Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
\hat H=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat h_i+\sum_{i<j}v_\mathrm{int}(x_i,x_j),\qquad \hat h=-\tfrac12\tfrac{\mathrm{d}^2}{\mathrm{d}x^2}+\vext(x),
\end{equation}
is assembled as a sparse matrix of dimension $N_x^N$: the one-body part is a Kronecker sum, $\sum_i I\otimes\cdots\otimes h\otimes\cdots\otimes I$, and the interaction is diagonal. The lowest eigenpairs are found with the implicitly restarted Lanczos method (ARPACK~\cite{ARPACK} via SciPy~\cite{SciPy}). Each spatial eigenvector $\psi$ is multiplied by the spin product state $\chi$ defined by the spin string, and the antisymmetriser $\sum_P(-1)^P\hat P$ is applied explicitly over all $N!$ permutations of the space--spin coordinates. Spatial states of the wrong exchange symmetry vanish and are discarded, so the $k$th surviving state is the $k$th fermionic eigenstate with the given $S_z$; the spin string fixes $S_z$, not the total spin, so for \texttt{"ud"} the excited states include the $S_z=0$ triplets. The number of Lanczos eigenpairs needed is estimated heuristically from $N$, the spin string and $k$, and can be set explicitly.

Time propagation in the presence of a perturbation $\delta v(x,t)$ uses
$\Psi(t_{j})=\exp[-\mathrm{i}\,\delta t\,\hat H(t_{j})]\,\Psi(t_{j-1})$,
which is exact for a time-independent Hamiltonian and first order in $\delta t$ otherwise. On the CPU the action of the exponential is evaluated with the algorithm of Al-Mohy and Higham~\cite{AlMohy2011}. Optionally, Hamiltonian construction, diagonalisation (CuPy's Lanczos solver) and propagation (a scaled Taylor series) run on a GPU through CuPy~\cite{CuPy}. In single-run benchmarks distributed with the code, an NVIDIA RTX~4090 reduces the two-electron ground-state solve on a 450-point grid from about 260~s on an Intel i9-11900K CPU to about 17~s.

\paragraph{Approximate methods}
Non-interacting electrons, Hartree, restricted and unrestricted Hartree--Fock (HF)~\cite{Fock1930}, the LDA and a hybrid share one self-consistent single-particle solver. For each spin channel the Hamiltonian $h_\sigma[n,\rho_\sigma]$ is diagonalised densely, and the density $n$ and spin-resolved density matrices $\rho_\sigma(x,x')$ are mixed linearly (default mixing 0.5) until $\int|n-n_\mathrm{old}|\,\mathrm{d}x$ falls below a tolerance (default $10^{-10}$). There is no iteration limit, and systems prone to charge sloshing need smaller mixing; the examples below use 0.2. The restricted option is intended for closed shells. Each method supplies its potential, namely $\vH$, $\vH+v_\mathrm{x}^\mathrm{HF}$, $\vH+\vxc^\mathrm{LDA}$ or $\vH+\alpha v_\mathrm{x}^\mathrm{HF}+(1-\alpha)\vxc^\mathrm{LDA}$ (default $\alpha=0.8$), and a total-energy expression. The LDA is the exchange--correlation energy of the spin-polarised 1D homogeneous electron gas parametrised in Refs.~\cite{Entwistle2016,Entwistle2018}, evaluated on the total density, so it is appropriate for fully spin-polarised systems. Excited configurations are formed by re-occupying the self-consistent orbitals, ranked by the sum of orbital energies; they are not separately self-consistent. Time-dependent versions (TDHF, adiabatic LDA, etc.) propagate the occupied orbitals with the same exponential, rebuilding the Hamiltonian from the density and density matrix of the previous step. An experimental implementation of KS strictly-correlated electrons~\cite{Malet2012,Malet2013,Mirtschink2013,Marie2022}, contributed by D.~Kooi, uses the same solver but needs the interaction and its derivatives as functions, is not yet part of the common interface, and can fail for densities with long low-density tails.

\paragraph{Reverse engineering}
For a target density $n_\mathrm{T}(x)$ and a chosen method, \iDEA{} finds the external potential of the fictitious system that reproduces $n_\mathrm{T}$ by iterating
\begin{equation}
v^{(i+1)}(x)=v^{(i)}(x)+\mu\left[n^{(i)}(x)^{p}-n_\mathrm{T}(x)^{p}\right]
\label{eq:re}
\end{equation}
until $\int|n^{(i)}-n_\mathrm{T}|\,\mathrm{d}x$ is below a tolerance (defaults $\mu=1$, $p=0.1$), a scheme used in earlier \iDEA{} work~\cite{Hodgson2013,Hodgson2016} and akin to that of van Leeuwen and Baerends~\cite{vanLeeuwen1994}. The power $p<1$ enlarges corrections where the density is small. With the non-interacting method and an exact $n_\mathrm{T}$ this yields the exact KS potential $\vks$, from which $\vxc=\vks-\vext-\vH$; the arbitrary constant is fixed afterwards, for example by requiring the highest occupied KS eigenvalue to equal minus the ionisation energy. In the time-dependent case, $\delta v(x,t_j)$ is found at each step by solving, with SciPy's Powell hybrid method, for the potential for which one propagation step reproduces $n_\mathrm{T}(x,t_j)$. The root finder's convergence is not enforced, so the returned per-step density error should be checked. This density-based scheme is practical on small grids; earlier \iDEA{} studies used a current-density-based variant~\cite{Hodgson2013}.

\paragraph{Observables and interface}
The \texttt{observables} module computes densities, density matrices, and Hartree and exchange potentials and energies from many-body states, single-particle states or their time evolution. Each method module provides \texttt{solve}, \texttt{propagate}, \texttt{total\_energy} and \texttt{hamiltonian}, so the single-particle methods can be looped over (their \texttt{solve} signatures coincide; the exact solver has its own options):
\begin{lstlisting}
import numpy as np, iDEA
x = np.linspace(-12, 12, 181)
s = iDEA.system.System(x, -2/(abs(x)+1),
        iDEA.interactions.softened_interaction(x),
        electrons="uu")
gs = iDEA.methods.interacting.solve(s, k=0)
n = iDEA.observables.density(s, state=gs)
for m in iDEA.iterate_sb_methods:   # NI, H, HF, LDA, hybrid
    st = m.solve(s, mixing=0.2)
    print(m.name, m.total_energy(s, st))
ks = iDEA.reverse_engineering.reverse(
        s, n, iDEA.methods.non_interacting, tol=1e-8)
v_xc = ks.v_ext - s.v_ext \
       - iDEA.observables.hartree_potential(s, n)
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Validation}

\input{figures/table_validation.tex}

The exact solver is tested against a semi-analytic reference (Table~\ref{tab:validation}a). For two electrons in a harmonic potential $\vext=\tfrac12\omega^2x^2$ with the softened interaction, the Hamiltonian separates into an analytic centre-of-mass part and a relative-coordinate problem, which the reference solves with the same stencil and grid spacing. Agreement to $\sim\!10^{-12}$ for both the spin-singlet (\texttt{ud}) and spin-triplet (\texttt{uu}) ground states therefore validates the many-body Hamiltonian, eigensolver and antisymmetrisation independently of discretisation error.
Discretisation error is controlled by the grid spacing: for the atom of Fig.~\ref{fig:gs}, whose potential has a cusp at the origin, the energy changes by about $10^{-3}$~Ha between $\delta x=\numconvdxB$ and $\numconvdxC$ (Table~\ref{tab:validation}c).
The harmonic-potential theorem~\cite{Dobson1994} gives two further tests. Interacting electrons in a harmonic well have a centre-of-mass (Kohn) excitation of energy exactly $\omega$, which \iDEA{} reproduces for the \texttt{uu} case ($N_x=151$, $L=20$) to within the small discretisation error that breaks exact separability. Under a uniform time-dependent field the interacting density moves rigidly, with a centre of mass that obeys the classical driven-oscillator equation; the exact propagation of two \texttt{uu} electrons reproduces this trajectory, with an error that halves when $\delta t$ is halved, as expected for the first-order treatment of a continuously varying field (Table~\ref{tab:validation}b). Finally, a single electron displaced in a harmonic well oscillates with an analytic density that the propagator reproduces at every step. The two-electron ground-state tests run in continuous integration; the remaining tests, and tests of the self-consistent methods (energy identities, agreement of restricted and unrestricted HF for a closed shell, and a reverse-engineering round trip), are run with \texttt{pytest}. The approximate methods are not compared with independent reference implementations.

\section{Examples}

All results below are produced by \path{paper/scripts/make_figures.py} in the repository, which regenerates every figure and number in this paper; timings in this section and Table~\ref{tab:validation}d are for 4 cores of an Intel Xeon (2.8~GHz) cloud machine, not the machine used for the GPU benchmarks above.

\paragraph{Ground state: comparing approximations}
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig1_densities.pdf}
\caption{(a) Ground-state density of two spin-polarised electrons in the softened atom $\vext=-2/(|x|+1)$ ($N_x=\numfignx$, $\delta x=\numfigdx$), exact and from each approximate method. (b) Error relative to the exact density (linear within $\pm10^{-3}$, logarithmic beyond).}
\label{fig:gs}
\end{figure}
Figure~\ref{fig:gs} shows two spin-polarised electrons bound by a softened nucleus of charge 2. The exact ground-state energy is $E=\numEexact$~Ha. HF ($\numEhartreefock$~Ha), the LDA ($\numElda$~Ha) and the hybrid ($\numEhybrid$~Ha) are close to it, whereas Hartree theory ($\numEhartree$~Ha) and non-interacting electrons ($\numEnoninteracting$~Ha) are not. The integrated density errors $\int|n-n_\mathrm{exact}|\,\mathrm{d}x$ are $\numdnhartreefock$ (HF), $\numdnhybrid$ (hybrid), $\numdnlda$ (LDA), $\numdnnoninteracting$ (non-interacting) and $\numdnhartree$ (Hartree). The self-interaction in the Hartree potential makes its density far too diffuse, worse even than ignoring the interaction. The LDA removes most of this error but still moves density from $|x|\approx2$ to $|x|\approx4$~\cite{MoriSanchez2008}, while HF, which is free of one-electron self-interaction, gives the most accurate density.

\paragraph{Exact Kohn--Sham potentials}
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig2_potentials.pdf}
\caption{Exact KS and xc potentials obtained by reverse engineering exact densities of the softened atom of Fig.~\ref{fig:gs}, with the constant fixed so that the highest occupied KS eigenvalue equals minus the exact ionisation energy, and shown only where $n>10^{-5}$. (a) Spin-polarised (\texttt{uu}) electrons, compared with the LDA xc potential of the same density. (b) Spin-singlet (\texttt{ud}) electrons, for which the exact exchange potential is $-\vH/2$, giving the exact correlation potential $v_\mathrm{c}$.}
\label{fig:ks}
\end{figure}
Reverse engineering the exact density of the spin-polarised atom (Fig.~\ref{fig:ks}a) gives the exact KS potential, and hence $\vxc$; the KS density reproduces the exact one to $\int|n_\mathrm{KS}-n|\,\mathrm{d}x=\numdnRE$. With the constant fixed by the exact ionisation energy ($\numipTriplet$~Ha), the exact $\vxc$ tends towards the expected $-1/(|x|+1)$ away from the atom ($\numvxcExactEight$ against $\numvxcAsymEight$~Ha at $x=-8$, where $n\approx\numnAtEight$); the residual difference reflects the slow approach to the asymptotic region and the finite box. The LDA, which depends only on the local density, decays far too quickly ($\numvxcLDAEight$~Ha) and is also too shallow at the centre ($\numvxcLDAZero$ against $\numvxcExactZero$~Ha). For the spin-singlet atom (Fig.~\ref{fig:ks}b; $E=\numEsinglet$~Ha) the exact exchange potential of two electrons in one spatial orbital is $-\vH/2$, so reverse engineering isolates the exact correlation potential, which lies between $\numvcMin$ and $\numvcMax$~Ha where $n>10^{-3}$. The same procedure, with another method in place of the non-interacting solver, gives exact Hartree- or HF-like fictitious systems.

\paragraph{Dynamics}
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig3_dynamics.pdf}
\caption{Dipole moment $d(t)=\int x\,n(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}x$ of the atom of Fig.~\ref{fig:gs} after a uniform-field pulse of strength $\numtdField$~a.u.\ and duration $\numtdPulse$~a.u., from exact propagation and from the time-dependent Hartree, HF, adiabatic LDA and hybrid methods ($N_x=\numtdNx$, $\delta t=\numtdDt$).}
\label{fig:td}
\end{figure}
Figure~\ref{fig:td} shows the free dipole oscillation of the same atom after a short field pulse. The exact propagation of $\numtdSteps$ steps takes about $\numtdTime$~s. Because the field is piecewise constant, the exact propagation does not depend on $\delta t$ here, whereas the approximate methods update their Hamiltonian explicitly and are first order in $\delta t$. Propagated through the same interface from their own ground states, the approximate methods differ in both frequency and amplitude: the adiabatic LDA oscillates too fast and time-dependent Hartree theory too slowly, while TDHF and the hybrid follow the exact dipole closely. The exact time-dependent density can in turn be reverse engineered to give the exact time-dependent KS potential~\cite{Hodgson2013,Hodgson2016}.

\section{Conclusions}

\iDEA{} provides exact many-electron solutions of 1D model systems, the standard approximate methods and exact reverse engineering of KS and other fictitious-system potentials, ground state and time dependent, behind a small Python interface. Because the wavefunction is stored on an $N_x^N$ grid, the exact solver is limited in practice to two electrons on a few hundred points or three electrons on a few tens of points (Table~\ref{tab:validation}d). This suffices for the reference calculations that motivate the code and lets them run interactively. The simple update of Eq.~(\ref{eq:re}) converges for the systems shown here, but its step size may need reducing, and it can stall for weakly coupled subsystems whose KS orbitals are nearly degenerate. More robust inversion algorithms~\cite{WuYang2003,Jensen2018,Kanungo2019,Shi2022}, convergence safeguards in the self-consistent and inversion loops, the many-body kinetic energy (currently unavailable for exact states), spin-resolved functionals and symmetry-adapted or tensor-network representations for more electrons are natural extensions. \iDEA{} is developed openly, and contributions are welcome.
